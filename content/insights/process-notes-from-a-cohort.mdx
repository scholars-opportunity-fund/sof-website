A recurring question we get from prospective analysts: what does the work
actually look like? This is an honest answer.

A typical week begins with the model's weekly digest, a ranked list of new
candidates scored across our special-situations feature set. Each analyst
picks one from the top of the queue and drafts a first-pass case within 72
hours. That case is not yet for the CIO. It is for the room.

## The internal review

The room is where the real education happens. Analysts present their
candidates to the rest of the cohort. Returning analysts run the session.
The goal is not consensus. It is to stress-test the weakest part of the
thesis until it either breaks or gets meaningfully stronger. Common patterns
we see get flagged early:

- **Confusing a catalyst with a mispricing.** Something happening is not the
  same as something being worth more than the screen says.
- **Undersizing the downside.** A memo that doesn't articulate the bear case
  with the same effort as the bull case isn't ready.
- **Hand-waving the base rate.** "Spinoffs outperform" is not analysis. How
  often, over what horizon, adjusted for what: that is analysis.

Memos that survive the room get cleaned up and forwarded to the CIO. Most
don't. That's the point.

## The standard

The standard we hold the memo to is whether it is fit to inform a real
capital decision. Not "is this a good undergraduate project." Not "is this
an interesting stock pitch." A real capital decision, in which being wrong
costs the fund and its LPs actual money.

Operating under that standard, every week, is the entire point of the
program. Most of what analysts learn at SOF is not a technique. It is the
feeling of work that is genuinely sufficient.
